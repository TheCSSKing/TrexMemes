🦖 Swarm Wars

How many small animals would it take to kill one giant one?
Twenty matchups. Rigorous* analysis. Zero animals harmed.

1. How many house cats would it take to kill a Tyrannosaurus rex?

Verdict: ≈ 2,500 cats
Tale of the tape — T. rex: ~8,000 kg, 12 m long, bite force ~35,000 N, top speed ~20 km/h. House cat: ~4.5 kg, 18 claws, vertical leap ~1.8 m, zero respect for authority.

The raw numbers look hopeless: a T. rex outweighs a cat roughly 1,800 to 1, and a single bite could delete a dozen cats at once. But the tyrant king has three fatal weaknesses: tiny arms that cannot groom cats off its own body, a tall body plan with vulnerable eyes and nostrils, and the stamina profile of an ambush predator — biomechanics studies suggest it couldn't sustain a chase for long without overheating.

Cats, meanwhile, are precision instruments: they climb, they cling, and they instinctively go for the face. Only 20–30 cats can physically engage the dinosaur at once, so this is a battle of rotation and attrition. Wave one blinds it — a T. rex's eyeballs are grapefruit-sized targets and utterly indefensible against a clawed animal on its snout. A blind T. rex then thrashes, stomps, and exhausts itself while relay teams open thousands of shallow wounds around the ankles, tail base, and throat, targeting the ankle flexors until it can no longer stand.

An 8-tonne animal that falls and cannot rise is finished; death comes from exhaustion, blood loss, and suffocation under a purring blanket. Expect catastrophic feline casualties — perhaps 60% — from bites, tail swipes, and stomping. Accounting for losses, rotation shifts, and a reserve force, you'd need about 2,500 cats. Realistically, 2,499 of them would wander off mid-battle, which is why the estimate includes a margin.

2. How many honey badgers would it take to kill a woolly mammoth?

Verdict: ≈ 120 honey badgers
Tale of the tape — Woolly mammoth: ~6,000 kg, 3 m tusks, thick fur over 2.5 cm hide. Honey badger: ~12 kg, jaws that crack tortoise shells, skin too loose to pin, famously does not care.

This is the most favorable pound-for-pound matchup on the list, because the honey badger is less an animal and more a grudge wrapped in leather. Honey badgers shrug off African porcupine quills, bee swarms, and cobra venom, and they attack lions on principle. A mammoth's defenses — trampling, tusk sweeps, and a prehensile trunk — all rely on pinning or striking, and the honey badger's absurdly loose hide means even a direct hit often just relocates an angrier badger.

The winning strategy writes itself: badgers dig and grapple at ground level, precisely where a mammoth cannot see over its own tusks. Teams of ten target each leg's Achilles region and the soft skin behind the knees, while others climb the wool like a rope ladder to reach the trunk — the mammoth's single most vulnerable organ, packed with blood vessels and essential for drinking. A mammoth that can't drink loses on a timescale of days; one hamstrung on two legs loses in an hour.

Sixty badgers could plausibly do the job over a brutal day-long siege, but mammoth stomps are genuinely lethal and some losses are unavoidable — call it 30% casualties. With attack teams, replacements, and a digging corps to open trench cover, about 120 honey badgers brings down the mountain of wool. The badgers would like it noted they would also have accepted worse odds.

3. How many chickens would it take to kill a Velociraptor?

Verdict: ≈ 5 chickens
Tale of the tape — Velociraptor (the real one): ~15 kg, turkey-sized, feathered, one 6.5 cm sickle claw per foot. Rooster: ~4 kg, bone spurs, flock tactics, a documented history of chasing humans off farms.

Surprise: this one is nearly a fair fight. The kitchen-stalking monsters of Jurassic Park were scaled-up inventions — the real Velociraptor mongoliensis was about the size of a large turkey, roughly 15 kg of feathered Cretaceous menace. Its weapons were real: a sickle claw on each foot for pinning prey, a long stiff tail for agility, and a mouthful of serrated teeth. But it was built to kill small mammals and juvenile dinosaurs, not to fight a coordinated flock.

Chickens are themselves dinosaurs — the closest living relatives of the raptor family among common farm birds — and they retain the hardware: spurs, flock mobbing behavior, and shocking aggression when a threat enters the yard. A lone hen loses instantly. But chickens mob. The raptor's sickle-claw pounce can only pin one bird at a time, and while it's occupied, two roosters flank it with spur strikes to the head and neck. Chicken losses are expected — the first bird pinned is probably not coming home to roost.

Give the flock five chickens — at least three of them roosters — and the raptor is overwhelmed: one bird sacrificed to the pounce, two flankers delivering spurs, two more piling on. Sixty-six million years of evolution, settled in a barnyard in ninety chaotic seconds.

4. How many brown rats would it take to kill a Smilodon?

Verdict: ≈ 1,000 rats
Tale of the tape — Smilodon fatalis: ~250 kg, 18 cm sabers, immensely muscled forelimbs, ambush specialist. Brown rat: ~0.35 kg, incisors that gnaw through concrete, can squeeze through a hole the size of its skull.

The saber-toothed cat is a glass cannon. Those iconic 18 cm fangs were surprisingly fragile against struggling prey — Smilodon used its wrestler's forequarters to pin victims before delivering a precise throat bite. Every part of that strategy fails against a swarm. You cannot pin a thousand rats. You cannot precision-bite a moving carpet. And each snap of those long sabers risks striking bone at a bad angle.

Rats bring three swarm superpowers: a bite that exerts enormous force for their size (rat incisors gnaw through cinder block), the ability to climb and cling to fur, and effectively unlimited aggression when cornered in mass. The swarm's strategy is pure attrition — hundreds of simultaneous bites to the belly, paw pads, tail, ears, and eventually eyes. A big cat's defense is swatting and biting, which kills perhaps two or three rats per second at peak fury. Sounds impressive, until you realize the cat tires in minutes while wave after wave keeps arriving.

Smilodon was an ambush sprinter with limited endurance. After ten minutes of frenzied swatting it is exhausted, lacerated in a thousand places, and losing blood faster than its body can compensate. Allowing for the several hundred rats it kills or maims before collapsing, about 1,000 brown rats ends the ice age's most famous predator. The rats' casualty officers report the losses as "acceptable, and honestly kind of fun."

5. How many piranhas would it take to kill a Megalodon?

Verdict: ≈ 15,000 piranhas
Tale of the tape — Megalodon: ~50,000 kg, 18 m long, bite force ~180,000 N, the largest predatory fish ever. Red-bellied piranha: ~1 kg, interlocking razor teeth, strips flesh in coordinated frenzy.

First, the awkward science: piranhas are freshwater fish and Megalodon ruled the open ocean, so we're staging this in a vast brackish estuary where both parties have agreed to appear. (Megalodon nurseries were in fact coastal and shallow, so it's less absurd than it sounds.)

A feeding frenzy of red-bellied piranhas can reduce a large animal to bone in minutes — each fish darts in, excises a bite-sized plug of flesh, and wheels away as the next takes its place. Against Megalodon, the swarm has two decisive targets: the gills, which are soft, blood-rich, and cannot be armored, and the eyes. Shark skin is a mail-coat of dermal denticles, tough enough to blunt most of the swarm's bites — but gill slits are an open door.

Megalodon's counterattack is genuinely terrifying: each gulp of that 2-meter mouth annihilates hundreds of piranhas, and its sheer swimming speed lets it burst away from the school to reset. But sharks must pass water over their gills constantly, and a gill curtain under continuous laceration means the giant is slowly suffocating even as it feasts. The math becomes a race: can the swarm bleed the gills faster than the shark eats the swarm? With ~3,000 piranhas lost per reset-and-feast cycle and several cycles before the gill damage becomes critical, roughly 15,000 piranhas are needed to drown the ocean's greatest predator in its own element. The estuary is not a nice place to swim afterward.

6. How many army ants would it take to kill a fire-breathing dragon?

Verdict: ≈ 2,500,000 ants (three full colonies)
Tale of the tape — Dragon: ~20,000 kg, flight, fire breath, scales hard as plate armor, likely hoards treasure. Army ant: ~0.005 g, shearing mandibles, no concept of retreat, marches in columns of hundreds of thousands.

A dragon is the perfect anti-swarm weapon — fire breath incinerates ants by the hundred thousand, armored scales are impervious to mandibles, and flight makes the whole battlefield optional. So how do ants win? The same way army ants kill everything: they don't attack the armor, they attack the openings. Nostrils, eyes, ears, mouth, and the soft tissue between scales at every joint. A dragon is a fortress, and every fortress has vents.

The battle is decided by one immutable fact: the dragon must sleep, and ants do not. A colony marches day and night in relays. Fire is devastating but self-limiting — a dragon torching its own legs, belly, and treasure hoard to clear climbing columns does the ants' work for it. Each landing, each sleep, each meal is an opening for columns to flow into the nasal passages and down the throat, stinging and shearing at airways and stomach lining. Death comes not from a wound but from ten thousand small occlusions: swollen airways, blinded eyes, inflamed joints between scales, and eventually suffocation mid-slumber.

One colony of ~700,000 is decimated by fire before achieving penetration. Two colonies force the dragon airborne indefinitely — but even dragons must land. Three colonies attacking in relays across a week, absorbing horrific flame losses, get columns inside the perimeter. Call it 2.5 million army ants. The dragon's hoard, for the record, now belongs to the colony, which has no use for it and will not be returning it.

7. How many corgis would it take to kill a griffin?

Verdict: ≈ 40 corgis
Tale of the tape — Griffin: ~250 kg, lion's body, eagle's head and talons, flight, guards gold. Pembroke Welsh corgi: ~12 kg, herding drive, ankle-height attack profile, monarchy-adjacent.

On paper the griffin holds every card: lion musculature, talons that can carry off a horse (per medieval sources, who admittedly also believed in barnacle geese), and total air superiority. But the corgi was literally bred for this exact fight: a low, fearless herding dog engineered to dart under the hooves of cattle fifty times its size, nip precisely, and be gone before the retaliatory kick arrives. A griffin is, tactically speaking, just a very fancy cow with opinions.

The flight problem dominates the analysis. Ten corgis cannot win because the griffin simply leaves, then dive-bombs stragglers — talon strikes from altitude are unanswerable, and the first swooping pass is where most corgi casualties happen. But herding dogs excel at exactly one dark art: controlling where a large animal is allowed to go. A pack of forty works in shifts — a bait ring of six lures the dive, then collapses inward the moment talons touch grass. On the ground, a griffin's eagle head has a blind cone directly beneath its beak, and its lion body has corgi-height hamstrings. Sprained wings can't generate liftoff with four dogs hanging from each.

Grounded, hamstrung, and encircled by relay teams that never stop barking (psychological warfare is free), the griffin succumbs to exhaustion and a hundred precision nips. Forty corgis — organized as five squads of eight — claim the treasure hoard, which they immediately ignore in favor of a nap in a sunbeam.

8. How many grey squirrels would it take to kill the Lernaean Hydra?

Verdict: ≈ 800 squirrels
Tale of the tape — Lernaean Hydra: ~9 heads (grows two per decapitation), poisonous breath, one immortal head, defeated only by Heracles with fire support. Grey squirrel: ~0.5 kg, ever-growing incisors, 3 m leaps, plans for winter.

The Hydra's famous defense — cut off one head, grow two more — is worthless here, because squirrels cannot decapitate anything. This sounds like a weakness but is actually the key to victory: the Hydra's entire regenerative arsenal is triggered by exactly the attack squirrels are incapable of performing. The monster is optimized against swords and utterly unprepared for rodents.

What squirrels do possess: incisors that grow continuously and must gnaw or overgrow, meaning a squirrel is biologically obligated to chew; tree-to-target aerial insertion with 3-meter leaps; and food-caching instincts that make them relentless, methodical hoarders of small victories. The battle plan is siege warfare from the swamp canopy. Squads drop onto each neck from overhanging branches — riding a neck it cannot see is safe from all eight other heads, which risk poisoning their own body if they breathe on it. Then the gnawing begins: eyes first (nine heads, eighteen eyes, ninety squirrel-minutes of work), then the tough but finite tissue of each neck, treated exactly like a walnut that fights back.

The poison breath claims whole squads — figure half the force lost to venom clouds and snapping jaws. But a blind Hydra swinging nine ruined necks cannot hunt, and the immortal head, while unkillable, can be buried under an enormous cache of stones — which is, conveniently, the one engineering project squirrels were born for. Eight hundred squirrels, give or take a bushel; Heracles needed a nephew with a torch, and the squirrels consider this fact very funny.

9. How many emperor penguins would it take to kill the Kraken?

Verdict: ≈ 5,000 penguins
Tale of the tape — Kraken: ~30,000 kg of cephalopod, ship-crushing tentacles, ink clouds, abyssal ambush predator. Emperor penguin: ~30 kg, dives to 500 m, underwater speed bursts, huddles through the worst conditions on Earth.

Everyone pictures the Kraken dragging ships to the deep, but consider its position here: it is fighting the only bird on Earth that out-dives it into its own ambush zone. Emperor penguins routinely hunt at 400–500 m depths in water that would kill most mammals, they see beautifully in near-darkness, and they are among the most maneuverable swimmers alive. The Kraken is used to prey that flails at the surface. Penguins commute to its house.

A giant cephalopod's weaknesses are well known to sperm whales, who eat its smaller cousins daily: the soft mantle, the eyes, and the fact that a squid's entire body is unarmored muscle. Tentacles are devastating against a ship — a single rigid target — but grasping individual penguins is like catching thrown darts with rope. Each tentacle strike claims a bird or two; each miss leaves the limb extended and exposed to a dozen beak strikes at the grasping clubs, where the tentacle is most delicate.

The colony fights the way it survives Antarctic winters: in coordinated, rotating waves. Squadrons of 500 dive in relays, targeting the eyes to blind it, then the siphon — the jet nozzle the Kraken needs both to breathe and to flee. Ink clouds and thrashing claim perhaps a fifth of the force, but a blind Kraken with a shredded siphon cannot hunt, jet, or oxygenate. Five thousand emperor penguins — one large colony, fighting in shifts between fishing breaks — retire the sea's oldest legend. They will accept payment in krill.

10. How many mallard ducks would it take to kill a Paraceratherium?

Verdict: ≈ 50,000 ducks
Tale of the tape — Paraceratherium: ~17,000 kg, 4.8 m at the shoulder, the largest land mammal ever, a hornless rhino shaped like a cathedral. Mallard: ~1.2 kg, flight, wing buffets, unlimited audacity, will bite you at a pond for no reason.

We saved the philosophical one for last. The mallard is the worst-armed animal on this list — no claws, no venom, a bill built for straining pondweed — and Paraceratherium is the largest creature ever to walk on land, a browser so big that adult individuals likely had no natural predators at all. No duck can meaningfully wound it. A million duck bites are a spa treatment. So how does the swarm win? It doesn't fight the body. It fights the schedule.

A 17-tonne herbivore lives on razor-thin margins: it must eat for most of its waking hours and drink enormous volumes daily. Ducks are the perfect blockade force — they own every waterhole, and they can relocate their entire army by air faster than the giant can walk. Fifty thousand mallards form a rotating harassment screen: wing-buffeting the eyes and ears whenever it lowers its head to browse, carpeting every water source, and maintaining a 24-hour quacking siege that makes rest impossible (a tactic banned by several conventions). The giant is never wounded — merely never allowed to eat, drink, or sleep again.

Megafauna of this size can starve in weeks but dehydrate in days. Duck casualties — from trampling, exhaustion, and simply forgetting the mission — run high in an operation this long, hence the inflated force size. Fifty thousand mallards, near-zero combat damage, one cathedral-sized herbivore defeated by pure logistics. The ducks call this "the siege of a thousand quacks." The ducks are extremely pleased with themselves.
